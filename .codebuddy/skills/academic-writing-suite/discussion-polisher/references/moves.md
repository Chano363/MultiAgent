# Discussion Section — Yang & Allison (2003) Seven-Move Framework

The most comprehensive and widely validated model for Discussion sections. Based on Yang & Allison (2003) and consistent with the textbook's closing section analysis.

---

## Move 1: Background Information

**Purpose:** Reorient the reader to the study context. Bridge from Results to Discussion.

- Brief restatement of the research purpose and/or the hypothesis
- Should not repeat the Introduction in full — 1–3 sentences maximum
- Tense: **past simple** (*"This study aimed to…"*, *"The purpose of this research was to…"*)

**Lexical signals:**
*The aim / purpose of this study was to… / This study investigated… / The present study examined…*

---

## Move 2: Reporting Results

**Purpose:** Briefly summarize the most important findings to anchor the discussion that follows.

- More concise than the Results section — select the key findings
- Do not repeat all results — only those that will be interpreted in Move 3/4
- Tense: **past simple**, active preferred
- This move is **obligatory** and appears with very high frequency

**Lexical signals:**
*The findings showed / revealed / demonstrated… / Results indicated that… / The data showed…*

---

## Move 3: Summarizing Results

**Purpose:** Synthesize findings into an integrated overall picture — what do the results add up to?

- Goes beyond individual findings to state what they collectively mean
- Uses present tense to make a current claim based on past findings
- Tense: **present simple** for the summary claim; **past** for individual findings cited

**Lexical signals:**
*Overall, these findings suggest… / Taken together, the results indicate… / In summary, the data show…*
*These findings are consistent with the view that… / The pattern of results suggests…*

**Common error:** Skipping Move 3 and going straight from Move 2 to Move 4. Without Move 3, the Discussion lacks a thesis — it is just a series of disconnected observations.

---

## Move 4: Commenting on Results

**Purpose:** The intellectual core of the Discussion. Interpret findings, situate them in the literature, and explain unexpected or contradictory outcomes.

This Move has four Steps, any of which may apply and may recur:

### Step 4a — Interpreting results
Explain what the results mean — beyond simply restating what was found.
- Tense: **present simple** with hedges
- *"These results suggest that female children may be more socially oriented in early play situations."*
- *"This finding indicates that X plays a more significant role than previously assumed."*

### Step 4b — Comparing results with prior literature
Position findings relative to existing studies — agreement or disagreement.
- Tense: **past** for what prior studies found; **present** for the comparison itself
- Agreement: *"These findings are consistent with / support the results of Smith (2020), who found…"*
- Disagreement: *"These results are contrary to / differ from the findings of Jones (2018), who reported…"*
- Always explain WHY when results differ from prior work

### Step 4c — Accounting for results
Explain unexpected or contradictory findings.
- Use hedges — these are interpretations, not established facts
- *"This discrepancy may be due to differences in sample composition."*
- *"The unexpected finding might be explained by…"*
- *"One possible explanation for this result is…"*

### Step 4d — Evaluating results
Assess the broader pattern or significance of the findings.
- *"These findings do not necessarily discredit prior research — they may reflect…"*
- *"Taken together, these results suggest a more nuanced picture of…"*

---

## Move 5: Summarizing the Study

**Purpose:** Draw an overall conclusion from the full set of findings and discussion.

- Restate the main takeaway of the study in light of all evidence
- Tense: **present simple** (current conclusion from past work)
- Voice: **active preferred**

**Lexical signals:**
*In conclusion… / To summarize… / Overall, this study demonstrates… / The present study has shown…*
*These findings provide evidence for… / This research contributes to our understanding of…*

---

## Move 6: Evaluating the Study

**Purpose:** Assess the research design — limitations, strengths, and methodological considerations.

### Step 6a — Indicating limitations
State specific constraints that affect the validity or generalizability of findings.
- Be specific: name the actual limitation (sample size, design, scope, measurement)
- Tense: **past simple** or **past perfect**; **passive preferred**
- Use hedges to show limitations reduce, not eliminate, validity
- *"The external validity of this study was limited by the small sample size (n = 20)."*
- *"The observational design precluded causal inference."*
- *"Generalizability may be limited by the homogeneous sample."*

### Step 6b — Indicating strengths / significance
Balance limitations with what the study contributes.
- Tense: **present simple** or **present perfect**, **active preferred**
- *"A key strength of this study is that it is the first prospective investigation of…"*
- *"The study contributes to the literature by…"*
- *"Unlike previous studies, this research…"*

### Step 6c — Evaluating methodology
Discuss specific methodological choices and their impact.
- Include only if methodology is central to interpreting results
- *"The use of observational methods allowed naturalistic data but precluded experimental control."*

---

## Move 7: Deductions from the Research

**Purpose:** Look forward — draw implications for theory, practice, or future research.

This Move is **obligatory in most disciplines** and is where the Discussion earns its significance.

### Step 7a — Making suggestions / recommendations
Provide concrete, actionable suggestions for practitioners, policymakers, or educators.
- Tense: **modal verbs** (should, could, may)
- *"These findings suggest that early childhood educators should incorporate…"*
- *"Policy interventions could target…"*

### Step 7b — Recommending future research
Specify what future studies should do — must follow from the limitations in Move 6.
- Always justify why the research is needed
- *"Future studies should include larger, more diverse samples to improve external validity."*
- *"A longitudinal design would allow researchers to examine whether…"*
- *"Future research could investigate whether these findings replicate in…"*

### Step 7c — Drawing implications
State what the findings mean for the broader field, theory, or practice.
- Tense: **present simple** with hedges; **modals** for possibilities
- *"These findings have implications for theories of early social development."*
- *"This work paves the way for further investigation of…"*
- *"The results suggest that the [theoretical model] may need to be revised to account for…"*

---

## Recurring Move Pattern

Moves 2–6 typically cycle across multiple findings:

```
[Finding A]
→ Move 2: Report finding A briefly
→ Move 3: Summarize what A means overall
→ Move 4: Interpret A / compare with literature / explain discrepancy
→ Move 6: Note limitation related to A

[Finding B]
→ Move 2: Report finding B briefly
→ Move 4: Interpret B / compare with literature
→ ...

[Overall]
→ Move 5: Summarize the whole study
→ Move 6: Overall limitations and strengths
→ Move 7: Implications and future research
```

## Obligatory vs. Optional Moves

| Move | Status |
|------|--------|
| Move 2 (Reporting results) | Obligatory |
| Move 4a (Interpreting results) | Obligatory |
| Move 4b (Comparing with literature) | Obligatory |
| Move 7 (Deductions) | Obligatory in most disciplines |
| Move 1 (Background) | Frequent |
| Move 3 (Summarizing results) | Frequent but often omitted |
| Move 5 (Overall summary) | Frequent |
| Move 6 (Evaluating study) | Frequent |
| Move 4c (Accounting for results) | Optional |
| Move 4d (Evaluating results) | Optional |

---

## Discussion vs. Results — Key Distinction

| Results section | Discussion section |
|----------------|-------------------|
| "Female children showed higher rates of play (M = 22.4 vs. 10.7)." | "This finding suggests females may be more socially oriented in early play." |
| "31% of participants drove unsupervised at least once." | "This high rate of non-compliance may reflect the normalization of risk among young drivers." |
| Raw data, statistics, observations | Interpretation, meaning, implications |

## Common Errors

- ❌ Repeating results in full instead of interpreting them (Missing Move 3/4a)
- ❌ No literature comparison (Missing Move 4b)
- ❌ Vague limitations: "this study has limitations" → specify them (Move 6a)
- ❌ "More research is needed" as the only future direction → specify what kind and why (Move 7b)
- ❌ No Move 5 — ending the section abruptly after discussing individual findings
- ❌ Overclaiming: "This proves that X causes Y" → hedge: "This suggests X may be related to Y"
