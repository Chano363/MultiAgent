  Abstract

- ### Abstract
 In this paper, a novel distributed braking scheme is proposed for automatic heavy-haul trains equipped with an electronically controlled pneumatic (ECP) braking system. The scheme consists of a coupler force compensator and a cooperative controller. The compensator is designed to counteract the coupler force acting on each car, i.e., the forces from its front and rear adjacent cars. With this compensation, the braking control problem is transformed into a platooning problem of multiple vehicles. The cooperative controller then regulates the velocity and position of adjacent cars. Numerical studies using MATLAB and the Universal Mechanism simulator are conducted to verify the effectiveness and superiority of the proposed scheme.

- ### I. Introduction

Heavy-haul railways are an efficient means of transporting resources and goods, such as coal, petroleum, and raw minerals, from inland regions to coastal areas. This mode of transport is particularly important for countries with vast territories and abundant natural resources, such as the United States, China, Canada, Australia, and South Africa. For instance, heavy-haul railways account for more than 60% of the total coal transportation in China.

At present, heavy-haul trains are operated by drivers with experience, as opposed to the automatic train operation (ATO) technology, which is widely used in metro subways and high-speed railways [1]-[3]. The target running curve for ATO can be generated through various methods [4], [5]. Although ATO technology holds promise for improving safety, energy efficiency, and punctuality, its application to heavy-haul trains is challenging due to the coupler forces between cars (locomotives and wagons) during the starting, coasting, and braking phases. This difficulty arises because heavy-haul trains are often several kilometers long and weigh tens of thousands of tons, leading to significant longitudinal impulses between cars, particularly during braking. This issue cannot be ignored. Therefore, effective braking control becomes increasingly crucial and challenging, attracting significant attention from researchers.

In the early stages, heavy-haul trains were modeled as a single-mass point system in [6]-[8] due to their relatively few cars and weight. The work in [6] solved the local energy minimization problem by designing an optimal train control strategy, [7] developed a fuzzy predictive control approach to reduce energy consumption during train operation while adhering to speed limits, and [8] proposed a robust adaptive sliding mode control strategy to guarantee internal stability and satisfactory tracking performance for the ATO system with unknown parameters, model uncertainty, and external disturbances. All of these works treat the entire train as a single mass. While this simplifies controller design and reduces computational complexity, it may pose a potential safety risk during braking due to the neglect of coupler forces between interconnected cars.

With the increasing length and weight of trains, braking control has been studied for more accurate models. For example, the multiple-point-mass model was first reported in [9], based on the linearization method for coupler forces [10]. Many control algorithms have been derived based on this model, such as open-loop control [11], [12], closed-loop optimal control [13], [14], and model predictive control (MPC) [15]-[17]. The open-loop optimal scheduling method in [11] and [12] for a heavy-haul train with coupler forces provides guidance for subsequent closed-loop designs. The work in [13] developed a closed-loop cruise controller to optimize state (displacement and speed) errors and inputs, demonstrating the advantages of the closed-loop controller compared to open-loop strategies such as optimal scheduling and heuristic scheduling. The reference [14] introduced adaptive fencing control technology to adapt to changes in track topology in practice. These techniques were also summarized in [18].

The aforementioned methods do not account for input constraints and model mismatches, which are influenced by locomotive power limitations and modeling accuracy. These issues were addressed using MPC in [15] and [16], where a penalized optimization approach was employed to improve the operation of heavy-haul trains. Subsequently, [17] introduced a Gaussian-like function to enhance train performance by dynamically adjusting the weight of the velocity tracking term in the objective function based on the current velocity tracking error.

However, MPC requires solving a quadratic programming problem using methods such as those described in [19]-[21]. This makes it difficult to meet the real-time computational and memory requirements for implementing the controller in long heavy-haul trains. In addition, the limited bandwidth of control signals in the communication system can lead to uneven coupler forces. In fact, all existing controllers based on a centralized (holistic) design scheme share these limitations and must be redesigned when car masses change. In such a scheme, braking control signals are computed centrally and then distributed to each car from the lead locomotive.

To overcome the shortcomings of centralized design, this paper adopts a new distributed scheme for designing local braking controllers, inspired by vehicle platooning technology [22]. Specifically, we introduce a coupler force compensator that counteracts the resultant force from front and rear coupler forces using fully actuated approaches [23], [24]. This compensation effectively eliminates the coupling effect between adjacent cars. As a result, the train model with coupler forces is reduced to a vehicle platooning model. Unlike conventional methods, the cooperative controller can then be synthesized individually for the resulting two-dimensional system, reducing computational complexity and communication bandwidth. Moreover, the controller design is independent of the car masses and exhibits robustness against aerodynamic resistance.

To implement the proposed distributed controller, two technical requirements must be met: i) the control signal must be computed by the local CPU in each car; and ii) the positions and velocities of adjacent cars must be obtained through sensors or communication devices such as cables or wireless systems. Nevertheless, this new design can serve as a technical foundation for ATO systems in heavy-haul trains, enabling increased speed and load capacity due to its distributed architecture. In summary, the proposed braking scheme provides the following key technical features: i) it operates by decoupling the coupler forces; ii) it requires lower communication bandwidth; iii) the controller for each car can be designed independently; iv) it is robust to variations in car mass and aerodynamic drag; and v) it enables smoother and safer operation in complex terrain.

The constants ${c}_{0},{c}_{v}$, and ${c}_{a}$ are general resistance coefficients. The term ${u}_{i}(t)$ represents the control input force, which specifically refers to the braking force in this paper.
