ABSTRACT 
The optimization of long-distance multi-objective operations for heavy-haul trains faces low solution efficiency when applying 
intelligent algorithms. Currently, few studies consider the changing emphasis of multiple operational objectives, such as 
safety, stability, punctuality and energy efficiency changes across different operational segments through task switching. To 
address this issue, this paper proposes a sequential multi-task collaboration hierarchical reinforcement learning framework for 
dynamic multi-objective optimization in long-distance operations. First, an event-driven time-dependent classification model 
is established to decompose the long-distance optimization problem into multiple subtasks, thereby reducing optimization 
complexity. Furthermore, a hierarchical reinforcement learning method based on sequential multi-task collaboration is designed, 
in which segment-specific subtask policies are trained in parallel and invoked sequentially along the route, while global 
coordination is achieved through a top-level dynamic programming framework. Simulation results based on a real 340-km railway 
line demonstrate that, compared to a single-agent global optimization method, the proposed framework improves convergence 
speed by nearly 40% and achieves superior optimization quality. It effectively restricts coupler forces within the safe limit of 1100 
kN, while successfully balancing multiple objectives including speed tracking and energy consumption. 
1 Introduction 
Heavy-haul trains (HHTs) play a critical role in modern freight 
transportation systems, particularly in the mining and bulk cargo 
sectors. With train configurations often exceeding 20,000 tons 
in weight and extending up to 2 km in length, their unique 
operational characteristics coupled with stringent safety and 
stability requirements pose significant control challenges when 
operating on long steep downgrades. 
Specifically, as large-inertia systems, HHTs accumulate substan- 
tial kinetic energy during descent, which cannot be sufficiently 
managed by electric braking alone, necessitating coordinated 
electro-pneumatic braking for effective speed control. With- 
out a well-coordinated braking strategy, there is a heightened 
risk of safety incidents such as overspeeding. Moreover, the 
extended train length leads to complex longitudinal dynamic 
behaviours, where uneven distribution of coupler forces may 
cause in-train oscillations or even coupler failures, directly 
jeopardizing operational stability and structural integrity. Addi- 
tionally, given the long-distance nature of heavy-haul routes, 
environmental conditions vary over time, requiring adaptive 
control strategies. Balancing multiple objectives, including safety, 
stability, punctuality and energy efficiency, has thus become 
a central challenge in enhancing the operational performance 
of HHTs. In recent studies, similar challenges in optimizing 
complex systems have been tackled using data-driven models 
[ 1 ].

Addressing the operational challenges of HHTs mentioned 
above, it is of great significance to conduct multi-objective 
operation optimization to reduce longitudinal in-train forces [ 2 ] 
and enhance multi-objective operational performance. Similar 
approaches have been applied in energy systems, where predic- 
tive control strategies are used to optimize load coordination, 
which has proven effective in improving system efficiency and 
stability [ 3 ]. Early research on train operation optimization 
primarily involved constructing a Hamiltonian function through 
equations of motion and objective functions, and solving the 
optimal control problem using variational methods based on 
Pontryagin’s maximum principle. In 1996, Howlett from the 
University of South Australia achieved the solution of this opti- 
mization problem under simple constraints [ 4 ], demonstrating 
the fundamental optimal regime sequence (full-power traction, 
constant speed, coasting and maximum braking), and further 
proposed the local energy minimization principle [ 5 ], proving the 
uniqueness of optimal switching [ 6, 7 ]. Recent scholars have fur- 
ther considered optimal connections under complex constraints, 
such as train traction system efficiency and pantograph power [ 8 ]. 
This method requires rigorous mathematical proofs. As the 
considered factors become increasingly complex, it becomes dif- 
ficult to apply to intricate optimization problems. Consequently, 
researchers have turned to mathematical programming-based 
algorithms to solve the speed profile optimization problem, 
transforming constraints and objectives into standard forms for 
mathematical programming algorithms (e.g., quadratic, linear 
forms) for solution. 
Without applying air braking, optimization methods primar- 
ily target gentle gradients, including model predictive control 
[ 9 ] and fuzzy control [ 10 ]. These methods iteratively calculate 
longitudinal forces during operation to solve for the optimal 
speed profile, thereby reducing longitudinal impacts and improv- 
ing average speed. However, suppressing longitudinal forces 
under cyclical air braking conditions on long, steep downhill 
sections is more challenging. Scholars have addressed this by 
establishing air brake models with simple functional forms and 
using programming algorithms to solve cooperative strategies 
for air braking and traction/electric braking control inputs [ 11 ]. 
Common methods include quadratic programming [ 12 ], mixed- 
integer programming [ 13, 14 ] and fuzzy control [ 15 ]. 
Although programming methods can achieve efficient solu- 
tions by formulating optimization models, considering complex 
objectives and models often necessitates model order reduction 
for high-order constraints and models, making it difficult to 
guarantee solution accuracy. This is particularly true for air 
brake systems, whose low-order models struggle to accurately 
describe time delays, propagation, and performance degradation 
characteristics. In light of this, some researchers have investigated 
the effects of various factors on air brake performance, such 
as brake shoe temperature [ 16 ], train formation [ 17 ] and brake 
cylinder filling rate [ 18 ]. Wu provided a review of different air 
brake models [ 19 ]. The aforementioned models are too complex 
for direct use within programming methods. In recent years, 
intelligent algorithms have become popular approaches for han- 
dling such optimization problems, as they do not require order 
reduction techniques, like linearization or complex mathematical 
derivations, and can directly incorporate complex models and 
constraints. These include, but are not limited to, various genetic 
algorithms [ 20–22 ], neural networks [ 23, 24 ], machine learning 
[ 25, 26 ], dynamic programming [ 27 ] and reinforcement learning 
(RL) [ 28–31 ]. Among them, reinforcement learning is widely 
applied in operation optimization as it can learn sequential deci- 
sions through continuous trial-and-error interaction in complex 
or even model-free environments. 
In recent years, reinforcement learning has achieved significant 
progress in the broader field of complex traffic and vehicle 
control. In particular, research on urban traffic signal control 
has gradually evolved from isolated single-intersection deci- 
sion making toward regional, hierarchical and heterogeneous 
cooperative control, thereby improving global traffic efficiency 
while enhancing local adaptability [ 32, 33 ]. Meanwhile, the 
optimization objectives of traffic control have expanded beyond 
conventional efficiency-oriented formulations to unified multi- 
objective reward modelling that explicitly incorporates low- 
carbon performance, safety and operational robustness. Recent 
studies have shown that distributed reinforcement learning can 
be used for cooperative low-carbon traffic network optimization, 
while signal control can also be coordinated with connected 
and automated vehicles to jointly improve safety and efficiency 
at intersections [ 34, 35 ]. In vehicle control, hierarchical rein- 
forcement learning and game-theoretic learning architectures 
have been increasingly adopted for autonomous driving tasks 
that jointly optimize energy consumption and traffic efficiency 
[ 36 ]. Overall, the research frontier is progressively shifting 
toward hierarchical reinforcement learning, distributed learning, 
multi-objective reward shaping under safety and efficiency con- 
straints and communication-efficient cooperative optimization 
mechanisms [ 37, 38 ], which provide important methodological 
support for the sequential multi-task collaboration hierarchical 
reinforcement learning (HRL) framework proposed in this paper. 
It is important to note that HHTs typically operate over large 
spatial distances with complex environments and train models. 
Using intelligent algorithms to solve such optimization problems 
often involves large solution scales and high dimensionality. 
Taking reinforcement learning as an example, when solving 
long-distance, large-scale optimization problems, its exploration 
mechanism often struggles to effectively capture long-term return 
maximization, easily falls into local optima and its performance 
can vary significantly across different solution attempts. 
Sequential multi-task collaboration provides a practical paradigm 
for solving such large-scale problems. It emphasizes coordi- 
nated learning and execution across multiple subtasks within 
a unified hierarchical framework [ 39 ]. By decomposing the 
global optimization problem into several coordinated subtasks 
and assigning each subtask a dedicated learned policy, this 
paradigm can significantly improve solution efficiency for large- 
scale problems and alleviate the curse of dimensionality [ 40, 
41 ]. Related task-decomposition and collaborative scheduling 
ideas have been widely applied in railway multi-train cooperative 
scheduling [ 42, 43 ] and urban traffic scheduling [ 44, 45 ]. The 
concept of self-organization in complex systems, such as the 
emergence of lane formations in bidirectional pedestrian flow, 
further suggests that local task-level coordination can induce 
globally desirable behaviour, which provides useful inspira- 
tion for railway operation optimization [ 46 ]. Although such

sequential collaborative task execution has not yet been applied 
to single-train long-distance operation optimization, these impor- 
tant innovations provide valuable research insights and solid 
empirical foundations. 
Based on the above analysis, this paper proposes a sequential 
multi-task collaboration HRL (MTC-HRL) framework suitable 
for long-distance operation optimization of HHTs. For clarity, the 
proposed framework organizes the route into segment-specific 
subtasks. The corresponding subtask policies are trained in 
parallel offline and invoked sequentially during online decision- 
making according to the train’s spatial progression, so the method 
is accurately characterized as a sequential multi-task collabora- 
tion mechanism. The aim is to generate optimal speed profiles 
and a combined air and electric braking strategy, balancing 
solution accuracy and computational efficiency. Addressing the 
differences in operation modes and optimization objectives across 
various operational scenarios over long distances, the problem is 
modelled as a set of segment-specific subtasks. Each subtask is 
associated with a dedicated policy for offline learning, and a top- 
level decision-making algorithm is designed to achieve optimal 
subtask allocation over the long distance, generating a globally 
optimized policy. The main contributions of this paper are as 
follows: 
1. A sequential MTC-HRL method suitable for long-distance 
operation optimization of HHT is proposed. Compared to 
single-agent deep reinforcement learning and other intelli- 
gent algorithms, it reduces problem complexity and improves 
solution efficiency while maintaining accuracy. 
2. A task classification model has been designed to distin- 
guish the operational modes between long steep downhill 
sections and undulating gradient sections. Different tasks 
are assigned tailored optimization objectives and differ- 
entiated weights, including speed tracking, energy effi- 
ciency and coupler force stability. Compared to traditional 
hierarchical reinforcement learning, the top-level dynamic 
programming-based optimal task scheduling avoids infeasi- 
ble objectives during training and simultaneously achieves 
dynamic multi-objective optimization for long-haul HHT 
operation. 
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 
describes the train model, task model and optimization model 
used. Section 3 details the design of the MTC-HRL method. 
Section 4 verifies the effectiveness of the proposed method 
through simulation case studies. 
2 Analytical Model 
This section first develops a dynamic model of HHT. It then 
analyses operational strategies for both long steep downgrades 
and rolling terrain, establishes an event-driven multi-task classifi- 
cation model, and formulates differentiated optimization models 
for distinct operational tasks.

2.2 Event-Driven Temporal Dependency Task 
Classification Model 
This section conducts a focussed analysis on the operational 
regimes of HHTs. In accordance with distinct control strategies, 
operational tasks are systematically categorized, and a task 
classification model is established to support subsequent targeted 
optimization approaches. 
Owing to their primary function in freight transportation, 
HHTs generally operate over extended distances with significant 
regional environmental variations, as illustrated in Figure 3 . 
Using a 20,000-ton HHT as a representative case, the following 
observations are made: 
On gentle track sections, the vertical profile is characterized by 
continuous ascending and descending gradients. Even though 
trains frequently pass through vertical curve points, the abso- 
lute gradient values are generally small, resulting in limited 
longitudinal forces on the train. Precise speed control can be 
achieved solely through adjustments in traction and electric 
braking, without the need for additional control forces. Therefore, 
the operational mode under such track conditions is classi- 
fied as single traction/electric braking control. The operational 
objectives focus on meeting predetermined sectional travel time 
and average speed requirements, while maximizing the utiliza- 
tion of gravitational potential energy to reduce traction energy 
consumption. 
On steep downhill sections, the significant gradient generates 
considerable inertial forces that exceed the control capacity of the 
locomotive’s electric braking system alone. Therefore, air braking 
must be additionally applied to maintain effective speed regula- 
tion, leading to the definition of a combined electro-pneumatic 
braking mode under such track conditions, as illustrated in 
Figure 4 . During the initial phase of air brake application and 
release, the forces on each vehicle change with a time lag due 
to the propagation of the air wave, which can induce substantial 
longitudinal impulses within the consist. Consequently, the 
operational objective shifts toward maintaining train stability, 
emphasizing the optimal selection of air braking initiation timing

3 Sequential Multi-Task Collaboration 
Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning Method 
The proposed framework follows a sequential task assignment 
and execution collaboration mechanism, where segment-specific 
subtask policies are invoked in sequence rather than being 
executed concurrently in a shared environment at the same 
decision stage. 
This section first formulates the differentiated optimization 
problems under various subtasks as respective reinforcement 
learning problems. A sequential MTC-HRL framework is then 
designed to achieve coordinated optimization across subtasks, as 
illustrated in Figure 5 . At the top level, the global optimization 
problem is transformed into a multi-stage dynamic programming 
problem, where optimal subtasks are selected based on stage cost 
values. The bottom level is responsible for training reinforcement 
learning policies for each subtask; here, segment-specific poli- 
cies are trained independently while sharing a common state 
representation. By continuously training these subtask policies, 
the top-level cost is iteratively updated. When the bottom-level 
policies converge, the optimal subtask and its corresponding 
optimal policy are simultaneously derived, thereby achieving 
long-distance dynamic objective optimization.

The network architecture of the standard DQN requires 
outputting a 𝑄-value for each state-action pair, which is relatively 
inefficient and redundant to some extent. For instance, when 
a train operates on a gentle segment, multiple actions (such as 
traction or braking) may be similarly optimal, and their 𝑄-value 
estimates show little differentiation. Significant discrepancies 
in the value of different actions only emerge at points of 
gradient change or when the environment undergoes substantial 
variation. 
To improve efficiency, the duelling DQN is adopted by decom- 
posing the 𝑄-network into a value stream 𝑉( 𝑠) and an advantage 
stream 𝐴( 𝑠 , 𝑎 ) . The value stream represents the value of the state

advantage stream captures the advantage of selecting a specific 
action compared to the average action-effectively the deviation 
from the state value. This structure enables the network to learn 
the value of a state more effectively without requiring updates for 
every minor variation in action selection. When the environment 
is insensitive to actions, the advantage values tend to approach 
to zero, and the 𝑄-value estimation is primarily governed by the 
value stream, leading to more stable and efficient learning, as 
illustrated in Figure 7 .

Task 
Simulations were conducted for the same sub-section (20–40 km) 
under different initial speeds. With a speed limit of 80 km/h and 
without pneumatic braking, the resulting speed profiles and the 
corresponding control forces are shown in the Figure 10 . 
Under random initial conditions, the learned task policy consis-

initial phase, the policy tends to apply a larger traction force 
at lower speeds to achieve rapid acceleration towards the target 
speed, with a longer duration of traction observed at lower initial

with control inputs of smaller magnitudes. Eventually, the train

safety, energy efficiency and stability across varying initial states. 
In terms of reward outcomes, lower initial speeds correspond to 
longer recovery times for returning to the optimal policy, lower 
average speeds and consequently, lower cumulative rewards. 
4.3 Combined Air and Electric Braking Task 
Simulations were conducted for a long downhill section (0–
20 km) under different initial speeds, with the speed limit 
maintained at 85 km/h. The timing of air braking and the strategy 
for coordinating with electric braking force were optimized. The 
resulting speed profiles and the corresponding electric and air 
braking force performances are shown in Figure 11 . 
On long downhill sections, the learned task policy is highly 
sensitive to the initial state, with a notable divergence between the 
optimal strategies at low and high initial speeds. When the initial 
speed is low, the optimal policy employs sequential applications 
of air braking to control speed while ensuring compliance with 
brake release time constraints. In this scenario, the electric 
braking force must be adjusted frequently to complement the 
performance of the air braking system. Conversely, when the 
initial speed is high, the optimal policy shifts towards maintaining 
a prolonged braking state. Here, the electric braking force remains 
minimal in magnitude, thereby reducing the rate of speed 
reduction and achieving an extended braking distance.

The left figure depicts the optimization results for train speed

steep downhill sections. In response, the optimization strategy 
shifts to a combined braking task, primarily utilizing air brak-

downhill segment, the strategy reverts to combined braking. 
During the integrated braking phase, the electrical braking 
force primarily serves a compensatory role in the overall train 
operation. In non-integrated braking phases, the electrical brak- 
ing force is utilized to counteract track resistance to maintain 
constant speed, while also enabling energy-efficient coasting by 
leveraging track resistance. 
Over long distances, and subject to meeting switching conditions, 
the MTC-HRL framework primarily switches tasks based on 
changes in line resistance, achieving dynamic multi-objective 
optimization in response to environmental variations. In terms 
of performance metrics, during the combined braking task seg-

scenarios. Regarding performance metrics, the maximum tensile

5 Conclusion 
This paper addresses the issues of low computational efficiency 
and limited adaptability of existing intelligent algorithms in the 
long-distance multi-objective operational optimization of HHTs. 
A sequential MTC-HRL framework is proposed, which achieves 
a balance between optimization accuracy and computational 
efficiency through task decomposition, parallel subtask learning 
and sequential collaboration strategies. 
The main contributions of this study are summarized as fol- 
lows: 
1. Event-driven task classification and hierarchical optimiza- 
tion framework: An event-driven, time-dependent task 
classification model is established, decomposing the long- 
distance optimization problem into a combination of electro- 
pneumatic combined braking control tasks and single 
traction/electrical braking control tasks. A hierarchical 
sequential collaboration architecture is designed, in which 
segment-specific subtask policies are trained in parallel 
and coordinated through top-level dynamic programming 
during route execution. This hierarchical decomposition 
strategy significantly reduces computational complexity and 
improves convergence performance. Simulation results show 
an improvement of nearly 40% in convergence episodes 
compared to global training. 
2. Multi-objective coordinated control strategy: Differentiated 
objective reward functions and constraint conditions are 
formulated for different tasks. Based on the D3QN learning 
algorithm and through shared state space, smooth task tran- 
sitions and sequential collaborative decision-making across 
subtasks are achieved. Simulation results demonstrate that 
coupler forces are effectively limited within 1100 kN, and both 
the average speed and energy consumption align with actual 
operational conditions. This realizes a dynamic balance 
among multiple objectives, including safety, stability, energy 
efficiency and punctuality.

Based on the conditions of a real 340-km railway line, compared 
to the global optimization method using single-agent reinforce- 
ment learning, the proposed MTC-HRL framework exhibits faster 
convergence speed and superior optimization quality, confirming 
its suitability for large-scale, long-distance HHT operational opti- 
mization. 
Furthermore, based on the simulation results, the practical 
effectiveness of the proposed framework is influenced by several 
key hyperparameters. In particular, the segment length should be 
determined by balancing task complexity and training stability: 
overly short segments may cause frequent task switching and 
increased training complexity, whereas overly long segments 
may reduce the performance benefits of multi-task switching. 
Moreover, different reward weight settings may change the pref- 
erence among energy efficiency, speed tracking and longitudinal 
smoothness, thereby affecting the corresponding multi-objective 
trade-offs. Benefiting from its hierarchical and segment-wise 
collaborative structure, the proposed framework is expected to 
remain effective under moderate parameter variations and to 
exhibit better adaptability than a single global policy under 
moderate changes in gradients, speed limits, and load condi- 
tions. However, more substantial environmental variations may 
still require retraining or online fine-tuning, which will be 
investigated in future work. 
Future research directions include extending this framework 
to heterogeneous multi-locomotive coordination scenarios and 
designing more scenario-specific tasks to achieve efficient paral- 
lel optimization. In addition, future work may explore a control 
framework based on externally configurable weight parameters. 
This framework aims to decouple long-term system dynamics 
from task-specific reward functions, thereby enabling external- 
ized configuration of optimization objective weights.
